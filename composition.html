Why compose single cases

We propose a model organism, a fruit fly for accumulating knowledge on the phenomenon of organizational emergence and evolution. We choose this phenomenon because it is where our failure to accumulate shows most. Although "the average organization does not exist", and "significant insights can arise from studying the best or the worst of a population" (Daft & Lewin, 1990:6), lessons drawn from the extremes alone misdescribe the population they come from (Denrell, 2003). Accumulated knowledge must cover the whole distribution and create "a deeper understanding of how organizational forms come to be different and remain different through time" (Lewin & Volberda, 1999:529), to understand how "organization form[s] emerge instead of being specified in advance" (Daft & Lewin, 1993:iv).

Studies of single cases do not add up

Our field inherited a limit from economics: studies of single cases do not add up. When interviews and surveys showed that managers do not set prices and wages at the margin (Hall & Hitch, 1939; Lester, 1946), economists replied that "to be important ... a hypothesis must be descriptively false in its assumptions" (Friedman, 1953:14; Machlup, 1946). Machlup (1967) later gave three reasons not to study real firms: cost, observability and composition, since he "might not be able to rely on the composite results obtained from a firm-by-firm analysis" (Machlup, 1967:30). Cyert and March (1963:128) had already answered the first two: their computer model of one department store's pricing, built from interviews with its buyers, made the mechanisms observable and cost only machine time. Composition is the constraint he kept: such a model "has usually not been transferable to other cases" (Machlup, 1967:16). Organization Science's inaugural editorial accepted the same limit, setting "in-depth understanding of a few cases" apart from "generalizability" (Daft & Lewin, 1990:6). Our methods still pick a side: grounded theory and multiple-case designs keep the detail of a few cases (Eisenhardt, 1989; Gioia et al., 2013), while set-theoretic methods compose many cases by reducing each to a few conditions (Fiss, 2011).

Why qualitative research

We focus on qualitative research for three reasons. First, the limit binds hardest there. Qualitative findings rest on case detail, which existing ways of composing cases discard. Yet our field remains "largely a science of verbal reports" (Daft & Lewin, 1990:3), and LLMs can now read such reports at population scale. Second, forms that emerge instead of being specified in advance need categories that also emerge from the record. Third, accumulation is least settled there. For some, transparency is a required step toward "replicable and cumulative knowledge" (Aguinis & Solarino, 2019:1293), and knowledge "accumulates vertically, building higher and higher on tested foundations" (Pratt et al., 2020:5); for others, it accumulates horizontally, as "a stock of broad principles generated across many past situations, each of them unique" (Pratt et al., 2020:6). Costly methods and unobservable phenomena deny others the chance to replicate or extend a finding; results that do not compose cannot add to prior knowledge. Our model organism addresses all three constraints: LLMs reduce the cost of reading the record, researchers can inspect the discussions and pixel changes, and the instrument composes the coded cases and keeps their detail.

Humans and LLMs are differently bounded

Humans and LLMs are differently bounded. Humans trade detail about any one case for being able to compose knowledge of many cases (Ragin, 1987). When LLMs form concepts, they compress towards statistical efficiency and can drop fine-grained distinctions that humans keep (Shani et al., 2026). They are predisposed "to identify, replicate, and reinforce dominant language and patterns" (Jowsey et al., 2025:1) and are "wildly conservative (tied to past data) as they seek to reduce surprise" (Felin & Holweg, 2024:350). Abduction "starts with the identification of an anomaly, that is, an observation which cannot be explained by existing theories" (Goldenstein et al., 2026:10). Our instrument makes departures from the population model explicit by separating case coding from population composition. Each coder first reads only its own community's full record. When the population model returns, the coder checks it against that record and identifies where it needs to change.

This motivates a division of learning. Qualitative researchers aim "to generate novel theoretical insights" (Lê & Schmid, in Gioia et al., 2022:243) and "formulate the issues and troubles that we notice in the world as problems of social science" (Bechky & Davis, 2025:11). LLMs appear "(currently) unqualified to assess whether a conjecture represents a theoretical contribution" (O'Sullivan & Ravasi, 2026:17). We give the community coders a common procedure to follow (Grodal et al., 2021; Than et al., 2025), "so that results are comparable and knowledge can be accumulated" (Günther et al., 2026:863). The aggregator composes descriptions; judging whether a composition is a theoretical contribution stays with us. We remain responsible for interpretation and reflexivity.

The instrument: one model, detailed at every level

The instrument is a harness in which LLM agents read the record under explicit virtual context management (Packer et al., 2024). One coding agent per community reads that community's full record and codes its organizing; an aggregator composes the coded cases into a population-level qualitative model (Gioia et al., 2013; Grodal et al., 2021). The model returns to the coders, who identify where it fails their cases. The aggregator revises the model and identifies organizational forms by grouping communities that request similar changes. An agent need not hold everything at once: it keeps notes outside its context window, fetches the material a question needs, and no case is dropped to make room for another. Because one agent studies each community, the model can build a census of this curated population (Hannan & Freeman, 1977) while retaining the nuance needed to distinguish how organizational forms differ (McKelvey, 1975).

The community

Two fleet coders coded r/PlaceNL's 701 posts and comments, each a batch of whole threads, into 246 first-order codes tied to their quotes; its community coder read every thread's opening and grouped the codes into 21 second-order themes. r/PlaceNL's coding

The population

The aggregator composed the 705 coded communities, with 115,388 first-order codes and 7,844 second-order themes, into 197 third-order themes, 28 fourth-order themes and eight aggregate dimensions. The population model

The return

Our instrument lets the community coders challenge the population model with their case evidence. An initial iteration of the population model stated that communities keep their work by repairing changed pixels. The coder of r/PlaceNL (a community founded to coordinate the Dutch artworks) objected to it with its community's record: members agreed, "we are going to make blue canal houses and a pretty little windmill on our flag" (r/PlaceNL, post 636e4q, H68:19, our translation). Yet the community's own defense script kept erasing them: "who, oh who, will turn off the defense script? The little houses are all being wiped away" (r/PlaceNL, comment dfrvgvk, H71:40, our translation). The aggregator found similar evidence in 67 communities. From these objections, the aggregator revised the model: repair restores the form the keepers know, so a planned change is erased as damage until the keepers learn of it. r/PlaceNL's return

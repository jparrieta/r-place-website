# Why accumulate knowledge on organizational emergence

Our field struggles to build cumulative knowledge (Pfeffer, 1993; Davis, 2010). A model organism gives later studies a common baseline: each new account must show what it adds to, corrects or qualifies in what is already known.

## Claims faster than we relate them

Part of the problem is ontological. As March said, "God gave all the easy problems to the physicists" (Dong et al., 2017:12): organizations are complex, change while we study them, rarely allow experiments, and confound skill with chance. Another part comes from our practices. Our promotion systems institutionalize a "general call to publish or perish" (Aguinis et al., 2020:136), and our journals urge us to "examine outliers (Lewin [1992]) rather than central tendencies" (Daft & Lewin, 1990:6). Jointly, ontology and practice promote the "search for novelty rather than truth" (Barley, 2016:5). Our field therefore produces claims faster than it relates them: a problem of "disorganization, not amount" (Cronin et al., 2021:668).

## Why emergence and evolution

We choose this phenomenon because it is where our failure to accumulate shows most. "Significant insights can arise from studying the best or the worst of a population" (Daft & Lewin, 1990:6), but lessons drawn from the extremes alone misdescribe the population they come from (Denrell, 2003). Accumulated knowledge must cover the whole distribution and create "a deeper understanding of how organizational forms come to be different and remain different through time" (Lewin & Volberda, 1999:529), to understand how "organization form[s] emerge instead of being specified in advance" (Daft & Lewin, 1993:iv).

## Three constraints on studying real organizations

Machlup (1967) gave three reasons not to study real firms: cost, observability and composition, since he "might not be able to rely on the composite results obtained from a firm-by-firm analysis" (Machlup, 1967:30). Cyert and March (1963) had already answered the first two: their computer model of one department store's pricing made the mechanisms observable and cost only machine time. Composition is the constraint that remained. Our methods still pick a side: grounded theory and multiple-case designs keep the detail of a few cases (Eisenhardt, 1989; Gioia et al., 2013), while set-theoretic methods compose many cases by reducing each to a few conditions (Fiss, 2011).

## What a model organism adds

r/place addresses all three constraints. LLMs reduce the cost of reading the record, researchers can inspect the discussions and pixel changes, and the instrument composes the coded cases while retaining their detail. Because one community coder studies each community, the population model covers a census of the curated population (Hannan & Freeman, 1977) while keeping the nuance needed to distinguish how organizational forms differ (McKelvey, 1975).

The dataset is frozen, but the instrument can change: a later run can use another literature, broaden the census or test what a new mechanism adds to the baseline. This makes the model organism an "open-ended program of research" (Lewin & Volberda, 1999:529). A model organism does not forbid outliers or novelty. It gives them a baseline to be measured against.

## Why qualitative research

We start with qualitative research for three reasons. First, the limit binds hardest there: qualitative findings rest on case detail, which existing ways of composing cases discard. Second, forms that emerge instead of being specified in advance need categories that also emerge from the record. Third, accumulation is least settled there. For some, knowledge "accumulates vertically, building higher and higher on tested foundations"; for others, it accumulates horizontally, as "a stock of broad principles generated across many past situations, each of them unique" (Pratt et al., 2020:5–6). A shared record serves both.
